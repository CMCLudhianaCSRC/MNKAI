Three problems limit what those reports mean for our patients. [Click] The first is the material these systems were built from. Aim and objectives

02

The aims and objectives.

Aim

Aim 1. To test whether an artificial intelligence system for the assessment of skin disease agrees with the diagnosis of a dermatologist in patients attending this hospital.

Aim 2. To build a de-identified collection of dermatological photographs, with clinical data and histopathology where available, from patients attending this department.

Same department, same 16 months: the first 196 patients belong to both aims.

Aim 1 is validation: to test whether the system agrees with the diagnosis of a dermatologist in patients attending this hospital. Aim 2 is collection: to build a de-identified collection of dermatological photographs, with clinical data and histopathology where available, from patients attending this department. [Click] The two aims run together, in the same department over the same 16 months; the first 196 patients belong to both.

Objectives

Under Aim 1

1.To measure the agreement between the system's ranked list of three diagnoses and the dermatologist's diagnosis in 196 analysable consecutive consenting patients (primary objective).
2.To measure the sensitivity and specificity of the system's cancer check for skin cancer, at both of its pre-set levels (amber and red), against histopathology where a biopsy is done.
3.To compare the system's accuracy across Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI.
4.To measure how often the system declines to give a single answer, its accuracy on the cases it does answer, and whether its stated confidence matches its accuracy.
5.To compare, in the same patients, the agreement of version 1.0 of the system with that of version 1.1, which is trained additionally on DermaCon-IN, an Indian dermatology image collection, and fixed before the first patient is enrolled.
6.To determine the agreement between the disease group assigned by the system, such as infection, inflammation or tumour, and the disease group of the reference diagnosis.

Under Aim 2

7.To collect, over 16 months, de-identified photographs with clinical data and histopathology where available.
8.To record the range and frequency of skin conditions seen in this department, and the distribution of Fitzpatrick skin types.
9.To measure the proportion of photographs rejected as technically inadequate, and the reasons.

The system runs in parallel with clinical care and does not affect it, a design known as a silent trial. [Click] The top lane is usual care, exactly as now: the patient sees the dermatologist, who decides diagnosis and treatment, and a biopsy only if it is clinically needed. [Click] The bottom lane is the study. It runs for every consenting patient: a photograph goes to both versions of the system, and the output is stored for the study only. [Click] Between them is a wall. The output is not shown to the treating team at any point, not entered in the record, and not used to decide investigation, referral or treatment. That holds even when the cancer check raises its red flag on a lesion the dermatologist judged benign: the system is unvalidated, its red threshold was set so that about one harmless lesion in ten reaches it, and acting on it could lead to unnecessary procedures. [Click] Information flows only one way: the dermatologist's diagnosis comes down to be compared with the system, never the other way. As a safety measure, the supervisor reviews the number of red flags raised on lesions judged benign every three months, using totals only. No patient's management changes because of this study.

Speaker script. This is how the project began: a convolutional neural network, followed here on a real photograph of tinea. [Click] To a computer, the photograph is only numbers. This is the raised edge of the lesion, pixel by pixel; each number is the brightness of one dot, from 0 to 255. [Click] The network slides small windows, called filters, across those numbers. Each filter is a small set of weights, and it lights up where its pattern appears. The first layer picks out simple things: here an edge filter traces the scaly rim, and a colour filter finds the redness. [Click] Deeper layers combine those into texture, such as scale, and then into the shape of the whole patch, the ring of tinea. [Click] At the end, the network turns those patterns into a probability for each condition. The maps you see are classic filters run on this photograph, to show the idea; a real network learns its own filters, by being shown thousands of photographs whose diagnosis is known and correcting itself after each mistake. Speaker script. This is the whole system, followed on one real photograph of lichen planus; every number on this slide is what version 1.0 actually produced for it. PanDerm is the foundation model, a vision transformer trained by its authors on over two million images of skin disease from eleven institutions; we use it unchanged. [Click] The photograph goes into PanDerm, which cuts it into small patches and compares every patch with every other. [Click] What comes out is not a diagnosis but a description: a feature vector of 6,144 numbers, taken from three depths of the program. [Click] Those numbers go to five small classifiers, each trained on the same 148,076 public photographs, each starting differently. [Click] Each gives its own answer. Four put lichen planus first, between 0.19 and 0.39; the fifth puts eczema first. [Click] Their answers are averaged, which is ensembling: lichen planus 0.30. [Click] Then calibration. A probability from such a system is not trustworthy by default: it may state 90% and be right 60% of the time. Think of a weather forecast: when it says 70% chance of rain, it should rain on about seven days in ten; calibration makes the system's percentages honest in the same way. PanDerm is the professor who describes every photograph in detail; the five classifiers are students who learned to name the disease from those descriptions, and we average the five students. The statistical analysis, as fixed in the protocol before any data are collected.

G. Primary outcome

  • Top-3 agreement (primary): the dermatologist's diagnosis is among the system's three most likely, with an exact 95% confidence interval.
  • Top-1 agreement: the system's first diagnosis is the dermatologist's, with an exact 95% confidence interval.
  • Cohen's kappa: agreement beyond chance on the single most likely diagnosis, across the 29 conditions plus "not on the list".
  • Ranked list: scored whether or not the system declined to give a single answer.
  • Unit of analysis: the close-up photograph of one index lesion per patient; the site photograph is secondary.
  • Matching: to the 29 conditions by Appendix B, by someone who has not seen the output; not on the list counts as disagreement.

PROFORMA

The same proforma is used for both aims. Every consenting patient completes Sections 1 to 5, including the system output, which is stored by the application and not shown to the treating team; the system output of patients beyond the first 196 is used for objective 2. The last block of Section 2 applies to photographs entering the Aim 2 collection.

Section 1 — Screening and consent

1. The treating dermatologist identifies a patient meeting the criteria. Every patient screened is entered in the screening log, with whether eligible, approached and enrolled, and the reason for any exclusion or refusal.

2. The patient information sheet is shown in the application in English, Hindi or Punjabi.

3. Written consent is taken on the paper consent form, including the participant's separate choice on future use of the images. The investigator confirms in the application that consent has been taken and records the future-use choice; the camera cannot be opened until this is done.

4. A study number is allotted in sequence. The patient is referred to by this number only.

Section 3 — Photography

1. Room lighting is kept the same between patients; mixed daylight and tube light is avoided where possible.

2. The lesion is exposed and any medicine, powder or cosmetic cleaned off. Jewellery and clothing that would identify the patient are removed from view.

3. The main camera lens is used. The ultra-wide lens and digital zoom are not used. No attachment is fitted.

4. The camera is left in its default photograph mode. High-resolution modes, filters and beautification are turned off.

5. A site photograph is taken first, showing enough surrounding skin to place the lesion.

6. A close-up is then taken with the lesion filling at least half the frame.

7. The application checks each photograph's size, sharpness, exposure, glare and compression and prompts a retake if needed, recording the reason.

8. In patients with darker skin the camera often under-exposes the lesion. The preview is checked and exposure raised if the lesion looks unnaturally dark.

Section 4 — Data handling and confidentiality

1. Photographs are stored under the study number only. No name, telephone number or address is stored with the image or in any free-text field. Unit number however will be taken in order to update histopathology samples in the database

2. The list linking study numbers to patient unit number is kept separately in a password-protected file, available only to the principal investigator and the supervisor, and used only to retrieve histopathology reports.

3. Photographs are stored encrypted on an institutional computer inside the institution. No image is kept on a personal cloud service. The analysis laptop is kept in the department, its storage is encrypted.

4. Signed consent forms and the screening log are kept in a locked cabinet in the department, separate from the photographs. The application records only that consent was taken, against the study number.

5. If a participant withdraws, their data are deleted from the repository.

6. Photographs do not show the person's identity: a facial lesion is photographed only as a close-up in which the person cannot be identified. Any identifying mark such as a tattoo is kept out of view or masked before storage.

7. Handling of data follows the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023,23 and the directions of the Institutional Ethics Committee.

PROFORMA

Section 5 — Histopathology

1. Where a biopsy is done on clinical grounds, the histopathology number is written on the proforma at the visit.

2. The case goes into a pending register.

3. When the report comes, the diagnosis is entered against the study number and the basis changed to histopathology.

4. Where a report cannot be traced, the case keeps its clinical diagnosis and the basis is unchanged. These cases are reported separately.

5. Where histopathology differs from the clinical diagnosis, both are kept, histopathology becomes the reference standard, and the difference is recorded.

Section 6 — Labelling for Aim 2

1. Every photograph carries a diagnosis recorded by a dermatologist.

2. No dermatologist recording a diagnosis is shown the system output, since that would bias the reference standard and destroy the independence of the collection.

3. Technically inadequate photographs are excluded and the reason recorded.
